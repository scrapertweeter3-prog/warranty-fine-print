The lifetime in a warranty is not yours

Two carry bags, both $220, both from brands stamped LIFETIME GUARANTEE on the tag. Both straps tear in year six. One owner pays $162 out of pocket and one pays $15, and neither company broke a single promise. The difference is one line of fine print about proration, and it is worth more than any feature on the spec sheet.

Bag A's guarantee prorates over a ten-year service schedule. Year six means forty percent of the life is left, so the credit is $88 toward a replacement $220 bag. The owner eats the other $132, plus $15 to ship the dead bag back and $15 to receive the new one. Six years of ownership cost $382 all in, about $64 a year, under a guarantee that never said owner. It said lifetime of the product, and the product's lifetime is whatever their schedule table decides.

Bag B replaces the bag flat, any failure, company pays shipping both ways except the owner covers the first label at $15. Same price, same year-six failure, but six years of ownership cost $235 all in against A's $382. That $147 spread is the entire purchase decision, and none of it shows up in the product photos. A guarantee that prorates is a discount code that arrives after you already needed it.

The checklist beats the headline every time: whose lifetime the term measures, whether coverage splits manufacturing defects from wear, how long the prorate schedule runs, which direction the shipping labels flow, and whether a receipt is required to invoke any of it. A shorter headline guarantee with a flat replacement and company-paid round-trip shipping will outperform a bigger number prorated to nothing. Read the schedule table before the marketing.
